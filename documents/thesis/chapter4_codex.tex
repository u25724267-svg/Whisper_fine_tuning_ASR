\section{Introduction}

This chapter examines whether curriculum learning and data augmentation improve
Shona automatic speech recognition. It reports the completed Whisper Base study
and the matched Whisper Medium extension, including their evaluation on an
external speech corpus. Whisper is the pretrained speech recogniser adapted
in these experiments \citep{Radford2023}. The central findings are that Medium
achieves substantially lower error than Base, that the effect of augmentation
depends on the model and evaluation corpus, and that combining a curriculum
with augmentation does not consistently improve every outcome.

The results incorporate the migration from an NVIDIA RTX 4090 to a machine
with two NVIDIA A100 GPUs. Fresh A100 repetitions of the earlier Medium
controls allow the main Medium comparisons to use one training-hardware family.
The historical executions are retained separately to show what changed during
migration. The reporting cutoff is 16 October 2026: the main Medium comparison
contains 27 completed training runs with WAXAL and corrected FLEURS evaluations.
Medium's WAXAL zero-shot evaluation is still in progress and is explicitly
left pending. Tables aggregate runs by condition to avoid repeating extensive
per-seed listings; the accompanying evidence files retain the individual results.

\section{Evaluation and reporting conventions}

WAXAL is the corpus used for fine-tuning. Its speaker-disjoint v2 partition
contains 13,807 training, 1,715 validation, and 1,671 test recordings, from
113, 24, and 24 speakers respectively. No speaker occurs in more than one
partition. Validation WER selects a saved model, or \emph{checkpoint}, at an
epoch boundary; the test scores evaluate that selected checkpoint. An epoch
is one scheduled pass through the training data. Earlier speaker-overlapping
experiments are not pooled with these results: changes in speaker composition
and partition membership prevent their score differences from isolating the
effect of speaker overlap alone.

FLEURS is an external multilingual speech benchmark \citep{Conneau2022}.
The corrected Shona preparation supplies 393 validation and 925 test recordings.
These recordings are not used for the study's WAXAL fine-tuning or checkpoint
selection. ``External'' denotes a different corpus and collection process;
it does not establish that the recordings were absent from Whisper's original
pretraining. Base and Medium use identical reference-manifest hashes for each
evaluation split. A difference between WAXAL and FLEURS scores describes
performance on different populations, rather than identifying its acoustic
or linguistic cause.

Word error rate (WER) counts substitutions, deletions, and insertions in the
recognised transcription relative to the reference:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{WER}=100\frac{S+D+I}{N},
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of reference words. Character error rate (CER) applies
the same calculation to characters. Both are percentages, and lower is better.
Corpus scores pool edit counts; they are not averages of recording-level
percentages. Insertions allow either rate to exceed 100\%. WER counts an
incorrect word even when only one character differs, so CER can be much lower.
Neither metric directly measures meaning or intelligibility.

Unless marked otherwise, entries are the arithmetic mean and sample standard
deviation (SD) of seeds 42, 43, and 44. A seed controls random operations during
training; it does not create new evaluation speakers. SD describes variation
between these executions, not a confidence interval or statistical significance.
All models begin from pinned pretrained weights. Base C2/C3 and Medium C3
have identical WAXAL prediction hashes across their seed-labelled records;
their scores are shown once with a dagger ($\dagger$), without an artificial
zero SD. Medium C3 and Base C2 also have identical FLEURS predictions. Base
C3 has two distinct external prediction exports across three records; its
displayed external mean is marked $\dagger$ and is not treated as three
independent training outcomes. C4, C4R, and C7 each have only one seed.

Recognition tables consistently use the canonical item-prediction summaries.
Training-loop scores can differ slightly because they come from a separate
generation pass. A positive improvement is control WER minus treatment WER,
measured in percentage points (pp). Thus a reduction from 24\% to 23\% is
1 pp, or a 4.17\% relative reduction. Calculations use unrounded values.
No new statistical significance claim is inferred from the condition means.

\section{Hardware migration and the analysis groups}
\label{sec:migration}

The Base study and the first Medium runs were trained on the RTX 4090.
When that machine became unavailable, the remaining Medium work moved to two
A100-SXM4-40GB GPUs. Each GPU ran an independent experiment; this was not
two-GPU distributed training, and the batch size was not doubled. The original
Medium C0 seeds 42--44 and C1 seeds 42--43 were then repeated from the
pretrained checkpoint on A100. C1 seed 44 had already been trained on A100
and was reused. These five fresh runs did not resume the RTX optimiser state.

The main Medium analysis therefore uses the three fresh A100 C0 runs, fresh
A100 C1 seeds 42--43 plus the existing A100 seed 44, and the existing A100
C3/A2/A3/S2--S5 runs. This grouping was specified by hardware provenance
before the new outcomes. It neither selects the better result from each
device nor pools repeated seeds as additional independent observations.
The 27-run Medium analysis and the five historical RTX-trained controls are
kept separate throughout.

The scientific settings remain matched: three epochs, train and evaluation
batch size six, one gradient-accumulation step, learning rate $10^{-5}$,
500 warm-up steps, half-precision arithmetic, and gradient checkpointing
to reduce memory use by recomputing intermediate activations. Greedy decoding
chooses the most likely next token without beam search; Shona transcription
and a maximum generation length of 225 tokens are fixed. Data, ordering,
augmentation, and training blocks in the 27 selected Medium configurations
match their Base counterparts; model weights and architecture differ.

models, and the large gain should not be presented as such.

\section{Curriculum and augmentation results on WAXAL}
\label{sec:waxal}

\subsection{Conditions and consolidated results}

C0 uses clean speech in shuffled order. C1 applies SortaGrad: shorter
recordings first in epoch one, then shuffled training \citep{Amodei2016}.
C3 orders recordings by a fixed combination of duration and estimated
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), which compares estimated speech energy with
background energy. These quantities are difficulty proxies, not direct
measurements of linguistic difficulty. A2 applies the fixed composite
waveform-augmentation policy with random ordering; A3 applies the same
policy with C3 ordering. This policy perturbs the waveform using the
configured noise, room-response, and tempo operations; it does not identify
their separate contributions.

S2/S3 apply light SpecAugment masking with random/SortaGrad ordering, and
S4/S5 apply stronger masking with those same orderings. SpecAugment hides
parts of the time--frequency input representation during training
\citep{Park2019}. The light policy (LB) uses one frequency mask of maximum
width 27 bands and one time mask of maximum width 100 frames. The stronger
policy (LD) uses two masks of each kind, with unchanged maximum widths.
Both allow a maximum time-mask proportion of 1.0 and omit time warping.
Masking is disabled at evaluation. Waveform and spectrogram augmentation
are separate interventions, not combined in one condition.

\subsection{RQ1: Curriculum learning}

For Base, SortaGrad reduces mean validation/test WER by 0.30/0.09 pp, but
slightly worsens test CER. C3 gives a larger test-WER reduction of 0.59 pp
and the lowest clean-curriculum test CER, 6.47\%; its validation improvement
is only 0.07 pp. The validation and test rankings therefore disagree.
For Medium, C1 and C3 are slightly worse than C0 on both mean validation
and test WER. C1 is worse on test WER at each matched seed; its mean
difference is only 0.04 pp. Medium C3 improves CER while worsening WER by
0.04 pp. A larger model consequently does not make the clean curriculum
policies consistently beneficial.

reference is under the model. C6 instead uses generated utterance WER, and
C7 combines acoustic and model-derived ordering. Historical configuration

Medium substantially improves external recognition. Clean C0 test WER falls
from Base's 58.31\% to 38.36\%, a 19.95-pp difference, and CER falls from
18.93\% to 9.98\%. Medium has lower external WER and CER in every shared

but remains substantial; the comparison does not identify which properties
of the two corpora account for it.

Base's clean SortaGrad condition has the lowest mean FLEURS test WER,
57.76\%, a 0.55-pp improvement over C0, although its CER is slightly worse.
Base C3 is 0.94 pp worse than C0 on external test WER despite its WAXAL
advantage. The newly available C2 and dynamic/pacing evaluations likewise
do not improve mean external test WER over C0. Thus the previous broad
statement that no curriculum improves FLEURS WER must be narrowed: Base
SortaGrad does so descriptively, but neither CER agreement nor a general
curriculum benefit follows.

The pattern changes with Medium. Clean SortaGrad worsens FLEURS test WER
by 0.49 pp and CER by 0.72 pp, despite a lower validation WER. C3 instead
improves test WER by 0.68 pp, although its WAXAL test WER is slightly worse
than C0. The curriculum's ranking therefore depends on both model and corpus;
the earlier Base C3 transfer failure is not a universal result for C3.

For augmentation, Medium A2 improves FLEURS test WER by 0.78 pp over C0,
with the favourable direction at all three seeds, whereas Base A2 worsens
it by 1.18 pp. Medium A2's test CER nonetheless rises slightly, from 9.98\%
to 10.08\%. Adding C3 to waveform augmentation does not improve the mean
external WER for either model. In Medium, A3 is also slightly worse than
clean C3 on external test WER, so its small in-domain gain does not establish
external transfer of that combination.

Light masking with random ordering (S2) provides the clearest change from
the earlier Base-only conclusion. It worsens Base FLEURS test WER by
1.36 pp but improves Medium test WER by 1.20 pp, giving the lowest Medium
external test WER, 37.16\%, and the lowest external test CER among its
shared conditions, 9.89\%. Each Medium seed improves test WER over its
clean control. The mean CER improvement is only 0.09 pp and is not uniform
across seeds, so the word-level finding should not be overstated.

Medium S3's lower WAXAL test WER does not make it the best external policy:
its FLEURS test WER is 0.36 pp above S2. Stronger masking also worsens
external test WER relative to the corresponding light policy, by 1.54 pp
for random ordering and 0.54 pp for SortaGrad. The condition with the lowest
Medium validation WER on WAXAL, S5, is therefore not the one with the
lowest external test error. These rankings describe frozen evaluations;
FLEURS test results are not used to retune the models.

The observed Medium transfer improvements resolve the earlier absence of
external results, but not uncertainty about generalisation. FLEURS has 348
distinct test sentence prompts, with repeated readings of some prompts.
Archived bootstrap analyses group recordings by prompt because speaker
identities are unavailable. This cannot account for unknown speaker dependence.
No new complete paired uncertainty analysis of the Medium comparisons is
claimed here, and three seed averages do not replace one.

\section{Training time, memory use, and learning behaviour}
\label{sec:resources}

The corrected RTX probes each completed two non-skipped optimiser updates,
after initial half-precision attempts were skipped, and then performed
generation evaluation. Allocated memory is storage occupied by active tensors;
reserved memory also includes storage retained by the software allocator.
These short probes establish bounded feasibility, not the largest allocation
over an entire training run. The earlier clean probe with no successful
optimiser update is excluded because it had not allocated comparable optimiser
state. The 14.99 GiB value must therefore not be described as a full-run
Medium peak.

The A100 measurements were recovered from local experiment-tracking records
for the five fresh controls. The assigned physical GPU was identified from
the lane schedule and recorded GPU identifiers; readings from the other
concurrent experiment's GPU were excluded. Memory was sampled approximately
every 15 seconds, with 1,681--1,849 readings per run. The recorded intervals
cover the tracked job, including training and final evaluation. Their maxima
range from 16.28 to 16.83 GiB. These are whole-device occupied-memory readings,
which can include driver and other process allocations; they are not
process-only tensor peaks and can miss short-lived increases between samples.

The completed runs and these measurements demonstrate that the unchanged
batch-six Medium protocol was feasible on the A100 machine. The two devices'
capacities were not pooled for one model. Comparable full-run Base memory
traces and process-level allocator peaks for all Medium treatments remain
unavailable in the inspected records. Consequently, the chapter reports
measured usage where available without inferring a model-wide memory ratio,
energy saving, or theoretical hardware-efficiency ranking.

\subsection{Checkpoint selection and learning behaviour}

All controlled runs train for three epochs, but their reported recogniser
is selected by validation WER, not necessarily from the final epoch.
The epoch boundaries are steps 2,302, 4,604, and 6,906. Validation loss
measures how well the model predicts reference tokens when preceding
reference tokens are supplied, whereas WER scores freely generated text.
The two measures can therefore move in different directions.

Base C5/C6 select epoch two: in subsequent training, validation loss falls
while generated WER worsens. This is not a universal epoch-two boundary
for all conditions; Base C0 and masking runs continue to improve their
validation WER. Medium waveform runs can likewise improve WER from epoch
two to three while loss increases. Thus lower loss alone does not establish
better recognition, and three epoch-level observations cannot locate an
exact onset of overfitting or prove a regularisation mechanism. The chapter's
accuracy comparisons consistently use the selected checkpoints.

\section{Interpretation, remaining limitations, and answers to the research questions}

The completed Medium matrix and external evaluations resolve several earlier
evidence gaps. Model-size conclusions are no longer based only on Base;
SortaGrad is no longer represented by one Medium seed; all masking cells
have final results; and Medium's external performance is measured. Fresh
A100 controls remove the earlier within-Medium training-hardware mismatch.
The new sampled memory evidence also extends the short feasibility probes,
although it does not supply uniform allocator measurements for every run.
These advances require revising the earlier blanket claims that waveform
augmentation is ineffective and that masking gains do not transfer externally.

For RQ1, no clean curriculum improves every reported endpoint across both
models and corpora. Base C3 favours WAXAL test recognition but worsens
FLEURS, while Medium C3 has the reverse WER pattern. SortaGrad gives small
Base WER gains, but does not improve Medium WAXAL test WER after the A100
control reruns. Dynamic Base curricula add no consistent accuracy benefit,
and generated-WER ordering is substantially more expensive.

For RQ2, light masking is useful in-domain for both models. Medium's
waveform-random and light-mask-random conditions also improve mean FLEURS
test WER, in contrast with their Base counterparts. Combining waveform
augmentation with C3 does not improve on waveform augmentation alone.
Combining light masking with SortaGrad gives the lowest Medium mean WAXAL
test WER, but worsens external WER relative to light masking alone.
Consequently, an in-domain ranking is insufficient for a claim of general
robustness, and the best choice depends on the stated evaluation objective.

Five limitations continue to constrain these conclusions. First, the findings
remain conditional on Shona, one WAXAL split, the pinned pretrained variants,
and a three-epoch training budget. Three seeds provide limited information
about training variability; identical deterministic predictions do not create
independent evidence. Second, WAXAL test results contributed to the historical
choice of C3 for the waveform study. Repeated inspection of WAXAL and FLEURS
does not constitute fresh confirmation, and unadjusted screening intervals
do not control the error rate across all comparisons. New Medium paired
intervals and formal interaction analyses remain outstanding.

Third, the composite waveform policy does not isolate noise, room-response,
or tempo effects, and the Base--Medium comparison changes pretrained weights,
architecture, and training hardware together. Fourth, the fixed 30-second
audio window retains full reference text even when longer audio is truncated.
Manifest durations exceed 30 seconds for 520 training, 21 validation, and
40 test recordings (3.77\%, 1.22\%, and 2.39\%). These counts identify
potential audio/reference mismatch, not its exact contribution to WER.
The separate 225-token generation limit has not received a matched sensitivity
analysis, and decoded output alone cannot establish whether the limit was
reached. Larger models and additional seeds do not resolve either restriction.

Finally, the missing Medium WAXAL zero-shot result prevents a complete
within-model estimate of its in-domain fine-tuning gain. Historical prediction
files are also not all present in the local mirror, although their summaries
and export records remain. Available prediction hashes, manifest identities,
edit counts, configuration matches, and sample statistics were checked for
this revision; these checks do not reproduce unavailable training or certify
every historical model weight. Full per-run values, source paths, missing-file
records, and memory-measurement provenance accompany the chapter in
\path{chapter4_per_run_codex.csv} and \path{chapter4_evidence_codex.json}.
